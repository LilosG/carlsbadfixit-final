## Understanding Your Current Setup

Before you buy anything, spend 20 minutes understanding what you have. Open the cabinet under your sink and look at the faucet connections. Are there two separate supply lines (hot and cold) or one unit? How are they attached—compression fittings, push-fit connectors, or solder? Take photos from multiple angles. Measure the distance between the holes where the faucet sits. For a toilet, note the bolt distance from the wall to the center of the flange.

Write these measurements down. Seriously. You'll forget them the moment you're at the supply store, and guessing is how you end up with fixtures that don't fit.

If your home was built before the 1980s, check for galvanized steel supply lines. These corrode from the inside and can restrict water flow. That's not a deal-breaker for a fixture swap, but it's worth knowing. ## Gathering Materials and Tools

You don't need a professional tool kit. Here's what actually matters:

**For faucet replacement:**
- Basin wrench (about $15, worth every penny)
- Adjustable wrench
- Phillips and flathead screwdrivers
- Plumber's tape (also called thread seal tape)
- Bucket or towel for water drips
- New faucet with supply lines included

**For showerhead or tub spout replacement:**
- Adjustable wrench or pipe wrench
- Plumber's tape
- New fixture

**For toilet replacement:**
- Adjustable wrench
- Wax ring (comes with most new toilets)
- Shims (plastic wedges to level the bowl)
- Putty knife or scraper
- New bolts and caps if not included
- Bucket for water in the bowl

Buy your fixtures at least a week before you plan to install them. Check the box for all components and instructions while you're still at the store. Nothing worse than discovering a missing piece on a Saturday when everything's closed.

## Prepping Your Workspace

Clear the cabinet under your sink completely. Move cleaning supplies, trash, everything. You need floor space to work and room to maneuver your hands. Lay down a towel or old sheet—water will spill, and you don't want it soaking into your subfloor.

For toilet work, clear the bathroom. You'll need space to move around the bowl, and you don't want kids or pets underfoot when you're dealing with wax rings and bolts.

Turn off the water supply. Find the shut-off valve under your sink (usually a small knob or lever on the supply line). Turn it clockwise until it stops. Then open the faucet to release pressure and drain any water in the lines. If there's no valve under the sink, locate your home's main water shut-off—usually near the meter or where the line enters the house. Know this before you start working.

## Removing Old Fixtures: The Real Steps

**Faucet removal** is where most people get stuck. Water will drip. The old connections might be corroded. The basin wrench is your friend here—it reaches up behind the sink and grips the faucet nuts without you having to contort your arm.

Loosen the supply line nuts with an adjustable wrench first. Have your bucket ready. Once the lines are loose, use the basin wrench to remove the faucet mounting nuts from underneath. The faucet will drop into your hands. Clean the sink surface with a scraper or putty knife to remove old caulk and mineral deposits. Wipe it down with a dry cloth.

**Showerhead removal** is straightforward: turn it counterclockwise by hand or with a wrench if it's stuck. Wrap the connection with a towel first to avoid stripping the finish. Clean the threads with a cloth.

**Toilet removal** requires more care. Turn off the water, flush to empty the bowl, then use a sponge or turkey baster to remove remaining water. Disconnect the supply line. Remove the two bolts at the base (they're usually plastic caps—pry them off first). Rock the bowl gently side to side to break the wax seal, then lift straight up. It's heavier than you think. Set it on cardboard or a tarp, not your bathroom floor—the wax ring will be messy.

If you're removing an old wax ring, use a putty knife. It's nasty work, but necessary. Scrape until the flange is clean and flat. If the flange is cracked or broken, that's a job for a [handyman in Carlsbad](/service-areas/carlsbad/) who can handle subfloor damage.

## Installing New Fixtures

**New faucet installation** is the reverse of removal, with one key step: apply plumber's tape to the supply line threads before connecting them. Wrap the tape clockwise around the threads three times. This prevents leaks at the connection.

Position the new faucet, tighten the mounting nuts from underneath, then hand-tighten the supply line connections. Use your wrench for a final quarter-turn—snug, not gorilla-tight. Turn the water back on slowly and check for drips. Small weeps at the connection are normal for the first few minutes; they usually seal themselves. If water streams out, turn off the supply and tighten another quarter-turn.

**New showerhead installation** is simple: wrap threads with plumber's tape and screw it on by hand, then use a wrench for the final turn. Test the spray pattern before you call it done.

**New toilet installation** starts with a new wax ring. Most come pre-attached to the bowl. If not, press it firmly onto the flange. Position the new bowl over the bolts, press down evenly, and tighten the bolts alternately (left, then right, then left again) to keep the bowl level. Use shims under the base if the floor isn't perfectly flat. Install the tank, connect the supply line, and test the fill and flush cycle.

## Caulking and Finishing

After your fixtures are installed and not leaking, let them run for 15 minutes. Check underneath again. If everything's dry, you can caulk around the base of the faucet or where the toilet meets the floor. Use silicone caulk (not acrylic—it'll crack). Apply a thin, smooth bead and let it cure per the product instructions before using the bathroom.
